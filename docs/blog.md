# The gap between seeing a puzzle and describing it

*Mohit Vaishnav and Tanel Tammet (Tallinn University of Technology), Halil Ibrahim Aysel and Meelis Kull (University of Tartu); Estonian Centre of Excellence in Artificial Intelligence (EXAI)*

An AI system struggles with a visual puzzle. Give it a written description of the
figures, and its answers improve. That result leaves a practical question: can the
system produce a useful description from the picture itself?

Our study, *From supplied structure to self-generated captions in visual rule
verification*, examines that gap. Across a broad model survey, descriptions
supplied from the programs that drew the figures improved performance. In
controlled experiments, models recovered much less of that advantage when they
had to write their own drawing recipes first. The way we measured an improvement
also mattered: a higher accuracy score could accompany a different tendency to
accept figures, or fewer correct answers returned across the full task.

## A puzzle with a known drawing process

A Bongard-LOGO problem presents six figures that follow a rule and six that do
not. The solver must infer the rule and decide whether a new figure follows it.
The rule might concern a shape, a spatial relationship or a combination of
properties.

Every figure is generated by a drawing program. This gives us several ways to
present the same problem: images, supplied drawing programs, descriptions derived
from those programs, or captions generated by a model looking at the images.

The distinction between those descriptions is essential. A program-derived
description provides structural information directly. A model looking at a picture
must recover useful information before its text can support the next reasoning
step. We used a shared inventory of 200 queries to compare these conditions, with
15 model configurations in the broader survey and four models in the controlled
caption-and-cue experiments.

## Useful supplied descriptions do not guarantee useful self-captions

Supplied descriptions improved accuracy in all 13 matched image–description
comparisons, with a median increase of 17.5 percentage points. Those comparisons
used questions answered under both representations.

We then asked models to caption each figure independently, without seeing its
rule or whether it belonged to the positive or negative examples. They used these
captions to solve the verification task. With step-by-step drawing recipes, three
of the four models recovered little of the supplied-description advantage in
discrimination. The fourth model's estimate was more sensitive to which questions
received usable answers.

Similar average accuracy also concealed changed answers: recipes fixed some
errors made from images while introducing other errors. A nearly unchanged score
therefore did not mean the model succeeded and failed on the same puzzles.

We also tested prose captions in two open-weight models. Prose had higher mean
discrimination than recipes across the four caption sets evaluated for each
model. The size of the advantage varied substantially between sets, however, and
uncertainty about future caption sets remained broad. Each protocol included one
greedy set and three sampled sets, so the comparison also reflects different
caption-generation settings.

![Prose-caption advantages across four sets](../analysis/patterns_outputs/patterns_caption_sets.png)

*Each set contains captions for every figure. Prose had the higher point estimate
in all eight model-by-set comparisons, but the size and uncertainty varied. The
four-set average describes the evaluated captions; it does not establish that
prose will outperform recipes across future sets.*

One possible explanation is that prose preserves useful relationships while
avoiding inaccurate drawing instructions. Our audits did not establish that
mechanism. Prose and recipes differed in content, length and structure, and simple
caption-fidelity measures did not consistently explain downstream performance.

## Accuracy depends on which questions get answered

Qwen3-VL:30b returned answers for all 200 image queries but only 120 description
queries. Descriptions improved accuracy on the questions answered in both
conditions. Across the complete inventory, however, the model returned 90 correct
answers from descriptions and 102 from images.

Both results matter: the first describes answer quality on a shared subset; the
second describes how many targets the system completed correctly. Missing,
malformed or non-terminating responses can change the set of questions behind an
accuracy score. Reporting answer coverage makes that difference visible.

## Saying “yes” less often is another kind of change

A verification task requires accepting valid examples and rejecting
counterexamples. Two solvers can achieve similar overall accuracy while making
very different mistakes.

![Similar accuracy, different response tendencies](../analysis/patterns_outputs/patterns_model_profiles.png)

*Some models with similar image accuracy lean toward acceptance; others lean
toward rejection. Separating performance on positive and negative queries exposes
this difference.*

We used signal detection theory to distinguish **discrimination**—how well a
solver separates examples from counterexamples—from **response criterion**, its
tendency to accept a figure. These measures describe behavior under explicit
statistical assumptions; they do not reveal a unique internal reasoning process.

In the model controls, correct rule cues gave higher discrimination estimates
than mismatched cues. Some mismatched cues also reduced willingness to accept.
Becoming more conservative was therefore insufficient evidence that a model was
using the correct rule. Controls that reduced overlap between the true and
mismatched labels weakened that response pattern.

A complementary study involved 207 human participants. The group shown the rule
rejected more counterexamples and spent longer on valid examples, while its
overall discrimination advantage remained uncertain. Because participation was
voluntary and the condition was disclosed beforehand, the groups may have differed
in who participated. This comparison does not isolate a causal effect of the cue,
and response times alone cannot establish how participants checked a rule.

## What to report alongside accuracy

For a visual verification experiment, we recommend four checks:

1. Compare the same questions across conditions and report acceptance of valid
   examples separately from rejection of counterexamples.
2. Report discrimination and response criterion, including their uncertainty and
   statistical assumptions.
3. Report answer coverage and correct answers across the full target inventory,
   alongside accuracy on shared answered questions.
4. Compare images, supplied structure and self-generated captions, using multiple
   caption sets and appropriate cue controls.

These checks helped us distinguish the benefit of receiving useful information
from the performance of a pipeline that must extract it. The evidence comes from
one visual benchmark and the tested models and protocols. Applying the same
checks to other tasks would test how broadly the findings transfer.
